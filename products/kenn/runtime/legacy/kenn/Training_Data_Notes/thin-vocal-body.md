# Thin Vocal Body

Type: Mixing workflow
Tags: vocals, thin, body, warmth, low mids, eq, compression, saturation, recording, mic technique, proximity effect
Status: Approved

Short answer:
Thin vocals can come from the recording (mic choice, distance, or a performer standing off-axis with no proximity effect) or from the mix (over-filtered low end, missing body, weak compression, or arrangement crowding the vocal's low mids). Rule out the recording stage first if you can — no amount of EQ fully replaces body that was never captured. If it's a mix-stage issue, restore low-mid body and stabilise level before reaching for brightness.

Try this:
1. If you can still access the source: check what mic was used, how far the performer stood from it, and whether they were on- or off-axis — a small-diaphragm condenser, a distant capture, or an off-axis angle all lose low-end body that EQ can only approximate, not recreate.
2. Turn off unnecessary high-pass filters and check whether the vocal body returns.
3. Listen around the low mids for body, using broad small moves rather than huge boosts — a large boost here is a sign the real problem is upstream, not that you found the right EQ move.
4. Use compression to stabilise the vocal before judging tone; inconsistent level reads as thinness even when the tone is fine.
5. Add gentle saturation if the vocal needs harmonic density, or layer doubles/harmonies quietly if the performance needs size rather than EQ.
6. Compare with a reference vocal at matched loudness so you do not over-thicken the mix.

Why it matters:
A thin vocal often sounds bright but disconnected from the track. If the cause is the recording (mic/distance/off-axis), that's worth knowing before spending time chasing it in the mix — a re-take or re-amp may be faster and cleaner than heavy processing. If the cause is in the mix, body and density help it feel expensive without making it muddy.
