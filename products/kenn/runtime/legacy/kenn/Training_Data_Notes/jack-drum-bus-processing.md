# Jack Gandy Drum Bus Processing

Short answer:
Jack Gandy's drum bus processing in Ableton Live relies on the native **Drum Buss** plugin combined with a **Transient Shaper**. He uses Drum Buss for saturation, compression, and sub-harmonic low-end weight, and a Transient Shaper to fine-tune the attack and sustain of the drum hits.

Try this:
1. Group all drum tracks (kick, snare, hats, percussion) into a single Drum Bus track.
2. Insert Ableton's Drum Buss plugin:
   - Set Drive to 10–20% (Soft mode) to add warmth and glue.
   - Adjust Compression to taste (usually 20–40% to squash and density the room sound).
   - Set Transient to 0 (default) or adjust slightly.
   - Enable the Boom control, setting it to the track's key (e.g. 50–65 Hz) and dial in 10–25% to add fundamental weight to the kick.
3. Insert a Transient Shaper after the Drum Buss:
   - Boost the Attack slightly if the drums are losing bite under compression.
   - Reduce the Sustain slightly if the room decay is muddying the gaps between hits.

Why it matters:
Drum Buss combines multiple processes (drive, compression, transient shaping, low-end boom) into a single stock tool, making it extremely CPU-efficient. Placing a dedicated transient shaper after it gives precise control over the envelope shape of the combined kit.

Common mistakes:
- Overprocessing the Drum Buss with too much "Boom", which creates muddy, uncontrolled low-end that conflicts with the bass.
- Forgetting to level-match the plugin output, leading to clipping.

When this does not apply:
- In minimalist acoustic genres (like jazz or classical) where clean, uncolored dynamic range is preferred over saturated punch.
