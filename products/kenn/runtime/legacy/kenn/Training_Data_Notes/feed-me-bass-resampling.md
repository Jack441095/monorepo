Short answer:
Build a personal bass library by bouncing Serum (or synth) patches through distortion and compression, then re-importing the audio into a sampler for further pitch, filter, and effect passes—bouncing often so you can step back to earlier iterations when a chain goes too far.

Key ideas:
- Resampling saves CPU and gives you an archive of original bass timbres you can reuse across projects.
- Bounce to audio, load into a sampler with seamless loop crossfades, then modulate pitch and filters while writing the part.
- Small pitch slides at note starts/ends add movement; distortion often feels harsher than compression for punch.
- Keep the low-mid band relatively clean on bass resamples—too much harmonic mud turns the sound into a noisy growl, not defined sub/bass.
- Around 4.5 kHz is where ears are most sensitive; if a sound feels oppressive at studio volume it will hurt on a club system—tame harshness before printing the next bounce.

Try this:
1. Design a bass in Serum (or your synth), add light distortion and compression, and scoop conflicting low-mids before the first bounce.
2. Freeze/flatten or export the loop, import into Simpler/Sampler, and set a clean loop with crossfades for sustained notes.
3. Automate short pitch envelopes at note on/off while writing the bassline.
4. Re-run the same processing chain on the resampled audio once; if it fails, revert to the previous bounce in your library.
5. Bounce again after each meaningful effect stage and label iterations so you can A/B less-processed versions.
6. Before the final commit, check level and harshness at normal monitoring volume, then high-pass non-bass elements in the mix.

Why it matters:
Iterative resampling turns one patch into many mix-ready bass textures without redoing sound design from scratch, and a dated archive of bounces speeds up arrangement when you need a known-good tone.
