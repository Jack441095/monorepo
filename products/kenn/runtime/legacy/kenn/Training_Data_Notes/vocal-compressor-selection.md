# Choosing A Vocal Compressor

Type: Vocal dynamics decision framework
Tags: vocal, compressor, compression, choose compressor, vocal compression, dynamics, transparent, character, leveling, mix
Status: Approved

Short answer:
Choose a vocal compressor by the job it must do, not by a “best vocal compressor” label. For transparent phrase leveling, start with a clean compressor that makes gain reduction predictable. For deliberate density or attitude, audition a character compressor only after the vocal is already controlled enough to compare fairly. If breaths, consonants, or harshness become more obvious, the choice or settings are solving the wrong problem.

Try this:
1. Level the loudest and quietest phrases with clip gain first. A compressor should refine remaining movement, not rescue an uncontrolled performance by crushing it.
2. Decide the job: catch occasional peaks, level phrases, add density, or create a deliberate pumping/urgent effect. Choose one main job for the first stage.
3. Compare two candidates at matched output level on the same verse and chorus. Listen for intelligibility, body, consonants, breaths, and emotional movement—not just apparent loudness.
4. For transparent leveling, prefer the candidate that keeps word starts and phrase endings natural while providing the required control.
5. For character, keep the candidate only if its color improves the vocal in the full mix without making upper mids, sibilance, or breaths persistently louder.
6. If no single compressor can catch peaks and level phrases cleanly, use light serial stages or automation; do not force one aggressive setting to do both jobs.

Listening checks:
- Bypass at matched loudness. The vocal should stay equally understandable while feeling more stable, not flatter or smaller.
- Compare a sparse section and the densest section. A good choice works in both, or tells you where automation is needed.
- Re-check after de-essing and ambience: a compressor can reveal sibilance, room, and breaths that were hidden before.

Avoid:
- Choosing a compressor only because it is famous or commonly used on vocals.
- Judging an un-level-matched plugin comparison.
- Using compression to compensate for bad mic placement, excessive room reflections, or masking from the arrangement.
- Driving fast release until every breath and consonant pulses between words.

Why it matters:
Vocal compression is a control and tone decision. The most musical choice preserves the performance’s intended movement while making the vocal dependable in the arrangement. Matching output and comparing several sections exposes whether a plug-in is genuinely helping or simply making the vocal louder.
