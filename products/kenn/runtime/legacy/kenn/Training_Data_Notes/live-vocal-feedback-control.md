# Stopping Vocal Feedback In A Small Live Venue

Type: Live-sound troubleshooting workflow
Tags: live sound, live venue, vocal feedback, feedback, microphone feedback, monitor wedge, PA, gain before feedback, ringing out, small venue
Status: Approved

Short answer:
Treat live vocal feedback as a gain-before-feedback and placement problem before reaching for a broad EQ cut. First lower or mute the affected vocal channel/monitor enough to stop the ring, identify whether the feedback is coming from the front-of-house PA or the performer’s wedge, then improve microphone/speaker geometry and only notch a confirmed ringing frequency. Protect hearing and the audience: do not sweep or boost aggressively while the system is live.

Try this:
1. Stop the feedback safely: pull down the affected vocal send or channel, then bring it back only after the system is stable. This preserves gain-before-feedback; do not keep raising gain against a ringing PA.
2. Identify the path. Mute the performer’s monitor send briefly: if the ring stops, investigate the wedge/IEM send; if not, investigate the main PA, mic position, and room coupling.
3. Keep the microphone’s rejection side facing the loudest monitor where its polar pattern permits. Move wedges out of the microphone’s most sensitive pickup area; do not point a speaker into the front of a cardioid vocal mic.
4. Reduce unnecessary monitor level before changing tone. Ask whether the singer needs more of themselves, less band, a different wedge position, or a lower stage volume.
5. Ring out only after placement and level are sensible: raise the relevant send gradually in a controlled check, identify one stable ring, and apply a narrow modest cut on that output path. Recheck before finding another frequency.
6. Keep the corrective EQ on the specific monitor or main path that feeds back. Avoid carving the vocal channel globally unless the same source resonance is genuinely the cause in every destination.

Verification:
The singer should hear enough vocal at performance level without a sustained ring returning when they move naturally around the microphone. The vocal should remain intelligible rather than thin from excessive notching.

Risks and alternatives:
Large boosts, broad cuts, or changing several EQ bands at once can provoke a louder ring or destroy vocal clarity. If placement and gain remain insufficient, use IEMs, a more directional microphone appropriate to the singer’s technique, lower stage volume, or revise the monitor mix rather than forcing more wedge level.

Why it matters:
Feedback is a closed acoustic loop between loudspeaker, room, microphone, and gain. Signal flow and geometry usually buy more stable gain than EQ alone, and output-specific correction prevents a monitor problem from degrading the front-of-house mix.
