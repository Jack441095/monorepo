# Choosing Reverb And Delay For A Lead Vocal

Type: Vocal space decision framework
Tags: lead vocal, vocal, choose reverb, choose delay, vocal reverb, vocal delay, plate, room, hall, slapback, tempo, depth
Status: Approved

Short answer:
Choose vocal reverb and delay by the role the space must play: keep a lead close but polished, place it in a believable room, create a long emotional tail, or fill gaps between phrases. Begin with one clear space on a send, compare it in the full arrangement, and use the shortest/cleanest effect that achieves the intended depth. The right choice preserves lyric intelligibility; it is not simply the lushest sound in solo.

Try this:
1. State the intended depth role before choosing a preset: short plate/room for glue and proximity, longer hall/chamber for distance or drama, tempo delay for rhythmic space, and throws for selected phrase endings.
2. Put the effect 100% wet on a return. Compare two candidates at the same return level while the full vocal and busiest arrangement play.
3. For a close lead, test pre-delay and a shorter decay before increasing wet level. The dry consonant should arrive clearly before the tail fills the gap.
4. For delay, begin with a musical division that supports the lyric rhythm. Reduce feedback and filter the return until repeats answer the phrase rather than compete with the next word.
5. High-pass and low-pass the return, then de-ess or dynamically duck it from the dry vocal if it masks the lead while it sings.
6. Automate long delays/reverbs for endings, transitions, and sparse moments instead of leaving the same wash active through every line.

Listening checks:
- Bypass at matched vocal level: the choice should create depth or rhythmic support without reducing lyric intelligibility.
- Compare a dense chorus and a sparse verse; a space that works only in the empty section probably needs automation.
- Check mono and low volume. The lead should remain centred and stable while the effect supports, not replaces, its presence.

Avoid:
- Choosing from a vocal preset in solo and assuming it will fit the arrangement.
- Inserting several reverbs and delays before defining the space each one serves.
- Using a long bright tail to compensate for a dry vocal that is actually too quiet, harsh, or under-compressed.
- Treating a fixed decay time or note division as correct for every tempo and lyric rhythm.

Why it matters:
Vocal effects establish distance, density, and emotion, but the same effects can bury words and blur a mix. Selecting them by role and judging them in the actual arrangement turns “more ambience” into a controlled musical choice.
