# Choosing Sounds For A Deep House Bassline

Type: Sound selection workflow
Tags: deep house, house, bassline, bass, sub, sound selection, low end, groove, synth, kick
Status: Approved

Short answer:
For a deep-house bassline, choose a bass sound that makes the rhythm and note changes readable without competing with the kick’s tail. Start with a simple, stable low-end source—often a controlled sine/triangle-derived sub with enough harmonics to read on small speakers—then add character only if the actual arrangement needs it. The correct sound supports the groove at low volume; it is not necessarily the widest, buzziest, or deepest patch in solo.

Try this:
1. Program the real bass rhythm and play it with the chosen kick before browsing patches. Sound choice depends on the pattern, register, and kick relationship.
2. Compare two or three bass candidates at matched level in the busiest loop. Listen for note definition, sustain, and whether the kick remains clear on every hit.
3. Start with a centred, mono-compatible low fundamental. Add a small amount of upper harmonic content only until the rhythm remains audible on a small speaker.
4. Shape the amp envelope so the bass releases before it blurs the next note or kick. A long release is a groove choice, not a default sign of “deep.”
5. Decide which element owns the deepest octave. If the bass owns it, choose a shorter/clearer kick; if the kick owns it, choose a bass sound with a controlled fundamental and useful upper body.
6. Check at low volume, in mono, and against one relevant reference at matched loudness. Keep the patch that makes the groove clearer across all three checks.

Listening checks:
- The bass notes should remain identifiable when the kick plays, without severe ducking or an unstable low end.
- The low end should remain centred and similar in mono; width belongs mainly in higher harmonic or supporting layers.
- If reducing bass level makes the groove clearer, the issue may be sustain, register, or arrangement rather than insufficient sub.

Avoid:
- Selecting a patch in solo and then trying to EQ it into the arrangement.
- Widening the fundamental frequencies to create size.
- Adding saturation or unison before verifying the kick/bass rhythm and envelope.
- Assuming every deep-house bassline needs the same “round” tone regardless of key, register, or kick pattern.

Why it matters:
Deep-house bass works as a rhythmic partner to the kick. Choosing its envelope, register, and harmonic audibility in context prevents the common cycle of boosting the bass, losing the kick, then over-ducking the groove.
