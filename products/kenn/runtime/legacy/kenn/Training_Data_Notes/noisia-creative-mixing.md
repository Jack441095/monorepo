Short answer:
Treat mixing as placement in frequency and stereo: separate kick and bass in the sub band, carve resonant areas with EQ while still mono, then use width, reverb, and mid/side balance so elements that would clash in the center can coexist when pushed to the sides.

Key ideas:
- Every sound has a fundamental (often sub/low) and a resonant presence (mids/highs); high elements rely less on a clear fundamental.
- Kick and bass fighting in sub is common—sidechain helps, but giving each its own EQ pocket (lower kick, less sub on bass) can be cleaner.
- Start mono to hear frequency clashes; accentuate separation with EQ notches and boosts before adding stereo effects.
- Creative reverb on bass can act like a musical layer (stab-like tails) and change the vibe of a loop, not only “space.”
- Wide synths with mono drums (or the inverse: stereo hats with mono bass/transients) changes what the listener focuses on—check mid vs side solo.
- In dance music, keep main drum transients largely mono; stereo interest can live in tails, verbs, and side content.
- Rhythmic elements that clash in the center (e.g. off-grid hat vs bass triplets) may work when panned wide apart.

Try this:
1. Build a simple kick, snare, and bass loop; fix sub overlap with EQ before relying only on sidechain.
2. Carve resonant bands per element (trim snare harshness, dip bass where kick needs body, boost upper bass for separation).
3. Add controlled stereo width (M/S EQ, reverb to sides, or duplicate layers) and A/B mono compatibility.
4. Solo mid and side to verify bass weight stays in mono while ear candy sits in the sides.
5. If two parts clash rhythmically in the center, try panning one wide or muting until the arrangement allows it.
6. Re-check the full loop: mixing choices are musical decisions, not only technical cleanup.

Why it matters:
Creative mixing is arrangement: frequency and stereo placement free up space so more elements fit without turning the master into a crowded center image.
