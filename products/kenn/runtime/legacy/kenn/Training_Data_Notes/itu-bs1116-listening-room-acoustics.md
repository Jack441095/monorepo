# ITU-R BS.1116 Listening Room Acoustics and Calibration

Type: Advanced studio setup reference
Tags: itu bs 1116, room acoustics, listening room, early reflections, reverberation time, rt60, noise rating, nr, background noise, monitor setup, calibration
Status: Approved
Source: Recommendation ITU-R BS.1116-3 — Methods for the subjective assessment of small impairments in audio systems (itu-r-bs-1116-3.pdf)
Reviewed: 2026-07-06

Short answer:
ITU-R BS.1116-3 defines the most stringent international standards for critical listening environments. It specifies optimal room dimensions, background noise limits (Noise Rating under NR10), early reflection attenuation (minimum -10 dB within 15 ms), and controlled reverberation times (T60) to minimize acoustic interference from the room itself.

Try this:
1. Measure your room's background noise floor. For critical listening, background noise must not exceed the NR10 limit (approximately 20–25 dBA) to prevent low-level details from being masked by ambient noise.
2. Control the room's reverberation time (T60) to stay within the recommended range of 0.2s to 0.4s for a typical control room size, keeping the response flat across the frequency range (especially 200 Hz to 4 kHz).
3. Place acoustic absorbers at early reflection points (sidewalls, ceiling, and desk) to attenuate early reflections by at least 10 dB relative to the direct sound path within the first 15 ms of sound arrival.
4. Position monitors symmetrically in the room so that the distance from the listening spot matches the distance between the speakers, forming an equilateral triangle.
5. Apply diffusion on the back wall to scatter late reflections, preventing discrete flutter echoes while maintaining an organic acoustic environment.

Why it matters:
When evaluating high-fidelity audio or small impairments (like lossy codecs or subtle EQ adjustments), room acoustics can easily mask or distort the sound. Adhering to the ITU-R BS.1116 guidelines ensures that what you hear is the actual audio signal, not the room's resonances or echoes.

Common mistakes:
- Neglecting low-frequency acoustic treatment (bass trapping), resulting in long decay times and severe comb filtering in the sub and mid-bass regions that skew leveling decisions.
- Ignoring structural background noise (such as HVAC rumble or computer fan noise), which masks the low-level tail of reverbs and fine detail in quiet passages.

When this does not apply:
- Standard office, home, or live sound environments where critical listening and analytical impairment assessment are not required.
